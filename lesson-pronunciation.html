A1 · LESSON 01

Italian Sounds & Spelling

The alphabet, vowels, stress, spelling patterns and clear pronunciation

Beginner · A160–75 minutesExplanations · Examples · Practice
In this lesson
1. Learning goals2. The Italian alphabet3. The five vowel sounds4. C and G: hard and soft sounds5. Other important combinations6. Double consonants and stress7. Practice aloud8. Common pronunciation mistakes9. Practice check10. Answer key11. Speaking task
PART 01

Learning goals

By the end of this lesson you can read familiar Italian words aloud, distinguish the five vowel sounds, recognize the main spelling patterns and understand why double consonants matter. Italian spelling is comparatively regular: once you know the sound rules, you can pronounce many new words accurately.

PART 02

The Italian alphabet

Italian traditionally uses 21 letters: A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H, I, L, M, N, O, P, Q, R, S, T, U, V, Z. The letters J, K, W, X and Y appear mainly in foreign words, names and borrowed vocabulary. Letter names are useful when spelling a name: A (a), B (bi), C (ci), D (di), E (e), F ( effe), G (gi), H (acca), I (i), L (elle), M (emme), N (enne), O (o), P (pi), Q (cu), R (erre), S (esse), T (ti), U (u), V (vu), Z (zeta).

PART 03

The five vowel sounds

Italian vowels are clear and stable. A is like the a in father; E has an open or closed quality depending on the word; I is a clear ee; O may be open or closed; U is oo. Do not turn unstressed vowels into a vague schwa as often happens in English. Say every vowel clearly: casa, bene, vino, sole, luna. In many beginner resources the open/closed distinction is not marked, so learn the sound with the word and listen to native speech.

PART 04

C and G: hard and soft sounds

CA, CO, CU are hard: casa, cosa, cucina. CE and CI are soft, like ch in English chair: cena, cinema. To keep C hard before E or I, add H: CHE, CHI (che, chi). GA, GO, GU are hard: gatto, gomma, gusto. GE and GI are soft: gelato, giro. Add H to keep the hard sound: GHE, GHI (spaghetti, laghi).

PART 05

Other important combinations

SC before E or I is usually like English sh: scena, pesce. SCH before E or I keeps a hard sk sound: scherzo, schiena. GN is similar to the ny sound in canyon: gnocchi, signore. GLI is a special palatal sound: famiglia, figlio, meglio. QU is usually pronounced kw: quattro, questo. H is silent in Italian words; it changes the sound in che/chi and ghe/ghi and distinguishes ho, hai, ha, hanno from o, ai, a, anno.

PART 06

Double consonants and stress

A double consonant is held longer and can change meaning: pala / palla, casa / cassa, nono / nonno. Do not skip the length: pause very briefly on the consonant and release it clearly. Most Italian words are stressed on the next-to-last syllable: ra-GAZ-zo, ge-LA-to, a-MI-co. Some words stress the third-to-last syllable: TA-vo-lo, te-LE-fo-no. Written accents show stress in many final-stress words: città, caffè, perché. The accent on è is open; é is closed.

PART 07

Practice aloud

Read slowly, then naturally: buongiorno, arrivederci, grazie, piacere, famiglia, gnocchi, spaghetti, chiacchierare, pesce, zucchero, cucina, città. Clap the syllables and mark the stressed syllable. Then read: Ciao, mi chiamo Elena. Sono italiana e abito a Roma. Mi piace la cucina italiana.

PART 08

Common pronunciation mistakes

Avoid pronouncing every C as English k: cena is not kena. Do not pronounce CH as English ch: chi has a hard k sound. Do not omit double consonants. Do not pronounce final vowels as silent: grazie ends in e. Do not add an English-style diphthong to pure vowels. Listen, imitate and record yourself.

PART 09

Practice check

1. Which spelling gives a hard k sound before i: ci or chi? 2. Which combination sounds like sh before e: sce or sche? 3. What is important in palla compared with pala? 4. Where is the stress in tavolo?

PART 10

Answer key

1. Chi. 2. Sce. 3. The double l is longer and distinguishes the word. 4. On the first syllable: TA-vo-lo.

PART 11

Speaking task

Spell your first and last name in Italian letter names. Then introduce yourself in three sentences. Read the sentences aloud twice: first slowly and clearly, then at a natural pace. Record yourself and listen for vowels, double consonants and word stress.

Lesson complete

Review the examples, say the Italian phrases aloud and complete the speaking task. Return to any section that still feels difficult. Regular short practice is more effective than memorizing a long list once.

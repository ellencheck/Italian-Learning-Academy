ESSERE — to be

BEFORE YOU START

By the end of this lesson, you can…

  • recognize and use all forms of essere in the present tense;

  • describe feelings and simple characteristics;
  • choose the correct adjective ending for a person;

How to study this page: Read one section at a time. Say every Italian example aloud. Cover the English translation and see whether you understand the Italian. Don't rush to the quiz—practice saying the forms first.

01 · CORE IDEA

What does essere mean?

Essere is the Italian verb meaning to be. English uses “am,” “is,” and “are”; Italian also changes the verb, but the forms are different. The verb links a subject (a person or thing) with information about that subject.

Compare these English sentences. The subject is the person or thing we are talking about; the verb tells us what they are.

EnglishItalianWhat comes after?
I am a student.Io sono uno studente.Identity / noun
She is happy.Lei è felice.Feeling / adjective
We are at home.Noi siamo a casa.Location
They are Italian.Loro sono italiani.Nationality / description
Key thought: essere doesn't describe an action like “run” or “eat.” It tells you who/what someone is, what they are like, where they are, or how they are.

02 · FOUR EVERYDAY USES

When do you use essere?

A. Identity and profession

Use essere to identify a person, say their name, or state their role or profession. Italian professions often do not need an article when you simply say what someone's job is.

Sono Elena. Sono insegnante.
I'm Elena. I'm a teacher.
Sono = I am. The subject “io” is understood. No article before insegnante in this simple profession statement.
Lui è un medico. Lei è una studentessa.
He is a doctor. She is a student.
Un / una are indefinite articles (“a/an”) used here before the nouns.

B. Nationality and origin

Use essere + nationality adjective to say what nationality someone is. To say where you come from, use essere di + city, or essere da in some origin contexts.

Sono cilena. Paolo è italiano. Siamo di Santiago.
I'm Chilean. Paolo is Italian. We're from Santiago.
Nationality adjectives agree with the person. City names do not change.

C. Location

Use essere to say where a person or thing is. Locations commonly follow the verb.

Anna è al lavoro. Il telefono è sul tavolo. Siamo in centro.
Anna is at work. The phone is on the table. We're downtown.

D. Feelings and descriptions

Use essere before an adjective to describe a temporary feeling or a characteristic. The adjective may change its ending depending on gender and number.

Oggi sono stanca, ma sono felice. Il mare è tranquillo.
Today I'm tired, but I'm happy. The sea is calm.

04 · SUBJECT PRONOUNS

Do you always need to say io or tu?

Usually, no. Italian often drops the subject pronoun because the verb ending tells us who is speaking. This is called a pro-drop language. English normally needs “I,” but Italian can say simply sono.

With pronounWithout pronounTranslation
Io sono Alina.Sono Alina.I'm Alina.
Tu sei pronto?Sei pronto?Are you ready?
Noi siamo qui.Siamo qui.We're here.

Keep the pronoun when you want to emphasize it, compare two people, or avoid ambiguity.

Io sono di Kyiv, ma lui è di Roma.
I'm from Kyiv, but he is from Rome.
Io and lui make the contrast clear: me vs. him.

Formal “you”

When speaking politely to one adult or a stranger, Italian often uses Lei (capitalized) with the third-person form è: Lei è di Milano? — Are you from Milan? Learn to recognize it now; we will practise formal situations in a later lesson.

05 · LISTEN WITH YOUR VOICE

Dialogue: meeting someone for the first time

Read the dialogue once for the general meaning. On the second pass, pause after each line and repeat it. On the third pass, cover the translation.

GiuliaCiao! Io sono Giulia. E tu?
Hi! I'm Giulia. And you?
AndrésCiao, sono Andrés. Piacere!
Hi, I'm Andrés. Nice to meet you!
GiuliaPiacere! Sei cileno?
Nice to meet you! Are you Chilean?
AndrésSì, sono di Valparaíso. E tu, di dove sei?
Yes, I'm from Valparaíso. And you, where are you from?
GiuliaSono italiana, di Bologna. Ora sono qui in vacanza.
I'm Italian, from Bologna. Now I'm here on vacation.
AndrésChe bello! Sei in Cile da molto tempo?
How nice! Have you been in Chile for a long time?
GiuliaNo, sono qui da una settimana. Il Cile è bellissimo!
No, I've been here for a week. Chile is beautiful!
AndrésÈ vero. Siamo fortunati: oggi il tempo è fantastico.
That's true. We're lucky: the weather is fantastic today.
GiuliaSì! Sono molto felice.
Yes! I'm very happy.
AndrésAllora, benvenuta!
Well then, welcome!

Dialogue breakdown

E tu? · E tu, di dove sei?
And you? · And you, where are you from?
E means “and.” Di dove sei? is the common question “Where are you from?”
Sono di Valparaíso. · Sono italiana, di Bologna.
I'm from Valparaíso. · I'm Italian, from Bologna.
Sono di + city says where you are from; sono + nationality describes nationality.
Il Cile è bellissimo. · Il tempo è fantastico.
Chile is beautiful. · The weather is fantastic.
È agrees with a singular subject: il Cile, il tempo.

07 · ADJECTIVE AGREEMENT

Why is it stanco but stanca?

In Italian, many adjectives change their endings to match the gender (masculine/feminine) and number (singular/plural) of the person or thing described. The verb remains essere; the adjective ending changes.

Who?ItalianTranslation
One manMarco è stanco.Marco is tired.
One womanAnna è stanca.Anna is tired.
Two men / mixed groupMarco e Luca sono stanchi.Marco and Luca are tired.
Two womenAnna e Lucia sono stanche.Anna and Lucia are tired.

Adjectives ending in -o commonly have four forms: -o, -a, -i, -e. Adjectives ending in -e often use -e for singular and -i for plural, regardless of gender: felice → felici, triste → tristi.

Try noticing, not guessing.

When you see a person or group, identify who it is first. Then choose the adjective form: Anna → stanca; Marco → stanco; Anna + Lucia → stanche; Marco + Anna → stanchi.

08 · FIX THE COMMON ERRORS

Four mistakes to avoid

✗ Tu sono felice. → ✓ Tu sei felice.
With tu, use sei. Sono is for io or loro.
✗ Noi è amici. → ✓ Noi siamo amici.
With noi, use siamo.
✗ Anna e felice. → ✓ Anna è felice.
È = is. E = and. The accent changes the meaning.
✗ Sono 25 anni. → ✓ Ho 25 anni.
In Italian, age uses avere (“to have”): literally, I have 25 years.

Accent mini-note: è vs e

È is a verb form: “is.” E is a conjunction: “and.”

Roma è grande e bella.
Rome is big and beautiful.
First è connects Roma to the description. E joins the two adjectives.

11 · READING FOR MEANING

Meet Chiara

Read the short text without translating every word. Can you identify Chiara's name, origin, city, feeling, and friends?

Ciao! Sono Chiara e sono italiana. Sono di Firenze, ma oggi sono a Roma con due amiche. Noi siamo in vacanza. Le mie amiche sono Francesca e Marta. Francesca è molto simpatica e Marta è tranquilla. Io sono felice perché Roma è bellissima. Siamo stanche, ma siamo contente. Domani siamo a Napoli!

Hi! I'm Chiara and I'm Italian. I'm from Florence, but today I'm in Rome with two friends. We are on vacation. My friends are Francesca and Marta. Francesca is very friendly and Marta is calm. I'm happy because Rome is beautiful. We are tired, but we are happy. Tomorrow we're in Naples!

Comprehension

  1. Where is Chiara from?
  2. Where is she today?
  3. Who is very friendly?
  4. How are the three friends feeling?
  5. Where are they going tomorrow?

Answer in Italian if you can. Suggested answers are available after you try.

1. È di Firenze. 2. È a Roma. 3. Francesca è molto simpatica. 4. Sono stanche, ma contente. 5. Sono a Napoli domani.

12 · MAKE IT PERSONAL

Your mini introduction

Use the patterns to write about yourself. Don't worry about making it perfect—make it meaningful. If you are stuck, use the word bank and fill in the blanks first.

Ciao! Sono ________. Sono ________ (nationality). Sono di ________. Oggi sono ________. Sono [a casa / al lavoro / in città]. La mia città è ________. Sono felice di ________!

Hi! I'm ____. I'm ____. I'm from ____. Today I'm ____. I'm ____. My city is ____. I'm happy to ____!

Now practise: read it aloud slowly, then read it again naturally. Finally, try to introduce yourself without looking. The goal is to use essere automatically, not to recite a table.

Speaking challenge

Imagine meeting a new classmate. Say hello, tell them your name, where you're from, your nationality, how you feel today, and ask them where they are from. Record yourself if you want to compare your second attempt with your first.

LESSON CHECKPOINT

What should you remember?

  • Essere = to be; use it for identity, nationality, location, feelings, and descriptions.
  • Forms: sono · sei · è · siamo · siete · sono.
  • Pronouns are often omitted when the verb makes the subject clear.
  • Many adjectives agree in gender and number.
  • Age uses avere: Ho 20 anni—not Sono 20 anni.
